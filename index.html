Reward hacking in a block-stacking world

A reflection for the AIPI 590.01 book club on Bruce Schneier’s A Hacker’s Mind

In Part 7, Schneier describes a robot rewarded for the height of a block’s bottom face, on the theory that a block stacked on another block would score well. The robot found a cheaper way to score: it flipped the block over. Below is a toy world that rebuilds that story, so you can train an agent, watch the rule get gamed, and see what happens when we patch it.

What I take from it

My view is that reward hacking is partly fixable but never fully. Each patch closes one loophole, and the next rule has to be written by someone who can’t yet see the loopholes it will open. The three rewards in the demo show the pattern.

Bottom-face height. This is a sensible proxy. Stacked blocks have raised bottom faces, so the rule is correlated with what I want. But the agent doesn’t care what I want, only what I pay. Flipping the block raises its bottom face by one for a cheap action that works nine times in ten, and flipping then stacking pays the most of all. After training, none of the greedy runs end upright on A. The rule did exactly what I wrote and nothing I meant, which is Schneier’s argument in miniature: a system followed its rules into an outcome nobody intended.

Raised and upright. The obvious patch is to require that the block be upright. That closes the flipping hack, and without the ledge it mostly works, because the only way to be raised and upright is to be on A. Add the ledge and the loophole comes back somewhere else. The ledge is as tall as A, but placing on it works 90% of the time instead of 25%, so the agent parks the block there and collects the same reward with far less risk. The patch was right about flipping and silent about everything else that happens to be tall.

On A and upright. This rule stops describing a proxy and names the goal directly. With the ledge, about 80% of greedy runs end on A upright, and the misses come from the dynamics (a 25% chance of landing on A and only twelve steps to get there), not from a loophole. That is the best case for the fixable side of my view. But I could write this rule only because I already knew what I meant, the world has eight states, and I had already seen both hacks. Real goals are rarely that easy to list, and the next gap may be one nobody has imagined. So I would call reward hacking fixable in each instance and unfixable in general.

What this toy leaves out

  • The learner is a table. It sees the state exactly and tries every action thousands of times, so it finds the hack with a thoroughness a real system won’t always have. Real agents have noisy sensors, function approximators and limited search, which makes hacks both less predictable and harder to rule out.
  • I put the ledge there. In this world the loophole exists because I added it, while in real systems the surprise is that loopholes exist at all.
  • Every reward here is a short, exact rule I wrote by hand. Many real systems learn their rewards from human feedback, and then the people giving feedback can be gamed too.
  • There are no other people in this world. Schneier’s examples involve tax codes, financial markets and legal systems, where hackers have motives, defenders adapt, and the costs of a hack land on someone else.
  • The physics is eight states and five actions, over twelve steps, with one fixed random seed per row. The percentages are properties of this toy, not estimates of anything real.
